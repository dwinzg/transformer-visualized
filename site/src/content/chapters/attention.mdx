  - Read a real attention grid, and say what it can and cannot tell you
minutes: 15
recap:
  - Attention lets each token pull in information from itself and the tokens before it.
  - A token's query is matched with every key using a dot product, giving one score each.
  - Softmax turns the scores into weights that add up to 1, and the token blends the values with them.
  - Each layer has several heads, and each head learns its own way of looking.

<Level of="story">

On its own, "her" does not say who. The model has to look back and find "Lily". <Term id="attention">Attention</Term> is how it does that (<Ref id="vaswani2017" />).

Each token looks at itself and the tokens before it, decides how much each one matters, and pulls in a mix of their numbers. In this head, "her" looks mostly at "Lily".

</Level>

<Level of="numbers">

Each row of the grid is one token, and its weights add up to 1. In layer 3, head 3, "·her" gives 0.66 to "Lily" and spreads the rest thinly.

The top right half is empty because no token can look ahead at the tokens after it.

</Level>

<Level of="formula">

Token <Tex tex="i" /> gets a weighted mix of the value vectors <Tex tex="\mathbf{v}_j" /> of the tokens up to it.

<Tex
  display
  tex="\mathbf{o}_i = \sum_{j \le i} a_{ij} \, \mathbf{v}_j, \qquad a_{ij} \ge 0, \quad \sum_{j \le i} a_{ij} = 1"
/>

</Level>

<Level of="code">

```ts
trace.layers[2].heads[2].weights; // layer 3, head 3, 6 × 6, each row adds up to 1
```

</Level>

</Step>

<Step title="Questions, name tags and backpacks">

<ToyAttention />

<Level of="story">

Each token makes three new lists from its numbers. Its <Term id="query">query</Term> is a question card, saying what it is looking for. Its <Term id="key">key</Term> is a name tag, saying what it has. Its <Term id="value">value</Term> is a backpack, holding what it will share.

A token compares its question with every name tag, and takes the most from the backpacks whose tags match best. Press Next to see each step.

</Level>

<Level of="numbers">

In the made-up example, "she" asks with (2, 0). Lily's tag (2, 0) scores 4, the tag of "ran" scores 0 and her own tag scores 2. After softmax, "she" takes 0.77 of Lily's backpack.

In our model each head's lists have 32 numbers, so the scores are divided by the square root of 32, about 5.66.

</Level>

<Level of="formula">

Stack the queries, keys and values of all tokens as the rows of <Tex tex="Q" />, <Tex tex="K" /> and <Tex tex="V" />. Then

<Tex
  display
  tex="\operatorname{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}} + M\right) V, \qquad M_{ij} = -\infty \text{ when } j > i"
/>

Each of <Tex tex="Q" />, <Tex tex="K" /> and <Tex tex="V" /> comes from the token vectors times a learned matrix, for example <Tex tex="Q = X W_Q" />. In our model <Tex tex="d_k = 32" />.

</Level>

<Level of="code">

```ts
const head = trace.layers[0].heads[0];
head.q; // queries, 6 × 32 (keys head.k and values head.v are the same shape)
head.scaledMasked; // scores divided by √32, with -Infinity where a token would look ahead
```

</Level>

</Step>

<Callout kind="analogy" title="Like a classroom">

Each student holds up a question card, everyone wears a name tag, and each student copies a little from the backpacks of classmates whose tags match. **Where this breaks.** Real students pick one helper. A token always takes at least a little from every earlier token, and it can only look at classmates who came before it.

</Callout>

<Step title="Softmax turns scores into weights">

<Level of="story">

Scores can be any size, even below zero. <Term id="softmax">Softmax</Term> turns a row of scores into weights between 0 and 1 that add up to 1.

It raises the number e, about 2.718, to the power of each score, then divides each result by their total. Bigger scores get a much bigger share, but every score that is not hidden gets at least a little.

</Level>

<Level of="numbers">

Take the scores 1, 0 and 0. e to the power of 1 is 2.718, and e to the power of 0 is 1. The total is 4.718, so the weights are 0.58, 0.21 and 0.21.

A hidden score counts as minus infinity, and e to the power of minus infinity is 0. So hidden tokens get a weight of exactly 0. This hiding is called the <Term id="causal-mask">causal mask</Term>.

</Level>

<Level of="formula">

<Tex display tex="\operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{z})_j = \frac{e^{z_j}}{\sum_k e^{z_k}}" />

</Level>

<Level of="code">

```ts
softmax([1, 0, 0]); // [0.576, 0.212, 0.212]
```

</Level>

<Exercise prompt="Turn the scores 2, 0 and 0 into weights. What is the first weight? Use e to the power of 2 = 7.389." answer={0.79} tolerance={0.01}>

e to the power of 2 is 7.389, and e to the power of 0 is 1, so the total is 9.389. The first weight is 7.389 divided by 9.389, about 0.79.

</Exercise>

</Step>

<Step title="Many heads, many layers">

/>

<Level of="story">

One way of looking is not enough. Each layer runs several <Term id="attention-head">attention heads</Term> side by side, each with its own questions, name tags and backpacks. They learn different jobs.

In layer 2, head 1 often looks at the token just before. In layer 3, head 3 looks back at "Lily" from almost every word. Their results are joined and passed on to the next layer.

</Level>

<Level of="numbers">

Our model has 4 layers with 4 heads each, 16 heads in all. Each head works with lists of 32 numbers, and the 4 heads together make 128 again. GPT-2 small has 12 layers (<Ref id="radford2019" />).

</Level>

<Level of="formula">

The heads' outputs are placed side by side and mixed by one more learned matrix.

<Tex
  display
  tex="\operatorname{MultiHead}(X) = \big[\,\text{head}_1 \;\cdots\; \text{head}_4\,\big] \, W_O"
/>

</Level>

<Level of="code">

```ts
trace.layers[1].heads[0].weights; // layer 2, head 1
trace.layers[0].attnConcat; // the 4 heads side by side, 6 × 128
```

</Level>

</Step>

<Callout kind="myth">

Attention weights show where a head looked, not why the model decided what it did. Researchers found that quite different weights can lead to the same prediction (<Ref id="jain2019" />). Others argued the weights can still be useful clues, if you test them carefully (<Ref id="wiegreffe2019" />). Treat the grid as a hint, not an explanation.

</Callout>
