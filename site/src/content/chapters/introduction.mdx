<Step title="It guesses the next piece of text">

<Level of="story">

When you type a message, your phone suggests the next word. A <Term id="language-model">language model</Term> does the same thing, only much better. You give it some text, and it answers with every piece of text that could come next, each with a chance.

</Level>

<Level of="numbers">

Here is what that answer could look like after "Once upon a". These numbers are made up to show the idea.

| Next piece | Chance |
| --- | --- |
| time | 0.92 |
| day | 0.03 |
| hill | 0.01 |
| everything else | 0.04 |

The chances, called <Term id="probability">probabilities</Term>, always add up to 1, because something has to come next.

</Level>

<Level of="formula">

The model assigns a probability to each possible next token, given all the tokens before it.

<Tex display tex="P(\text{next token} \mid \text{previous tokens})" />

For each position the probabilities over the whole vocabulary sum to 1.

</Level>

<Level of="code">

```ts
const trace = forward(model, tokenIds);
const lastRow = rowView(trace.logits, tokenIds.length - 1);
const chances = probabilities(lastRow, 1); // one number per token in the vocabulary
```

</Level>

</Step>

<PredictReveal question="Which word is most likely to come after &quot;The cat sat on the&quot;?" options={['mat', 'moon', 'because']} answer={0}>

Most people pick "mat", and a model trained on lots of English text usually does too, because that phrase is common in its training text. The model has never seen a cat. It has seen patterns in text. The playground lets you check what our small model predicts.

</PredictReveal>

<Exercise prompt="A model gives &quot;time&quot; a chance of 0.9 and &quot;day&quot; a chance of 0.05. How much chance is left for every other piece together?" answer={0.05} tolerance={0.001}>

All chances add up to 1, so what is left is 1 minus 0.9 minus 0.05, which is 0.05.

</Exercise>

<Step title="Then it does it again">

<Level of="story">

To write more than one word, the model picks one of the likely pieces, adds it to the text, and guesses again. Repeat this a few hundred times and you have a paragraph. Chatbots like ChatGPT write their answers this way, one piece at a time.

</Level>

<Level of="numbers">

Starting from "Once upon a", three rounds could go like this.

1. "Once upon a" gives "time" the highest chance. The text becomes "Once upon a time".
2. "Once upon a time" makes "," likely. The text becomes "Once upon a time,".
3. "Once upon a time," makes "there" likely. The text becomes "Once upon a time, there".

</Level>

<Level of="formula">

The probability of a whole sequence is the product of the next-token probabilities, one position at a time.

<Tex display tex="P(t_1, \dots, t_n) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(t_i \mid t_1, \dots, t_{i-1})" />

Generating text means choosing each <Tex tex="t_i" /> from that distribution and feeding it back in.

</Level>

<Level of="code">

```ts
const result = generate(model, promptIds, { maxNewTokens: 20, temperature: 0.8, seed: 1 });
```

</Level>

</Step>

<Callout kind="note" title="An old idea">

In 1951 Claude Shannon (<Ref id="shannon1951" />) asked people to guess the next letter of English sentences, one letter at a time, to measure how predictable English is. A language model plays the same game with pieces of words.

</Callout>

<Step title="Inside, a transformer does the guessing">

<Level of="story">

The model that makes these guesses is called a <Term id="transformer">transformer</Term> (<Ref id="vaswani2017" />). It turns the text into numbers, passes those numbers through a stack of layers that let each word gather information from the words before it, and ends with a score for every possible next piece. The next chapters open each of those steps.

</Level>

<Level of="numbers">

The small model on this site has a vocabulary of 4,096 pieces. It turns each piece into a list of 128 numbers, runs them through 4 layers, and has about 1.3 million numbers it learned during training. Large chat models follow the same plan with tens of thousands of times more numbers, or more.

</Level>

<Level of="formula">

A transformer computes scores for every token in the vocabulary, called logits, from the tokens so far. Softmax turns the logits into probabilities.

<Tex display tex="\text{logits} = f_\theta(t_1, \dots, t_{i-1}), \qquad P(t_i \mid t_{<i}) = \operatorname{softmax}(\text{logits})" />

Here <Tex tex="\theta" /> stands for all of the model's learned parameters.

</Level>

```ts

console.log(model.config); // { vocabSize: 4096, dModel: 128, nLayers: 4, ... }
```

</Level>

</Step>

<Callout kind="analogy">

Phone suggestions are a helpful picture, but the model does not look up its answer in a list of phrases it has seen. It computes each guess from numbers it learned, which is why it can continue sentences it has never seen before.

</Callout>
